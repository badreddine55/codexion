## Test 5 (`200 5000 200 200 200 3 0 fifo`): **should not have burned out, but did**

Margin here is huge on paper — cycle = 600ms, burnout budget = 5000ms, over 8x slack. This should complete cleanly, same as test 2's logic just scaled up. Instead, **coder 92 burns out at t=5001**, never having compiled even once.

Look at the actual timeline: by **t=4830**, the log is still only reaching coders in the **~85-91** range for their apparent *first* "has taken a dongle." That means after 4830ms, fewer than half the 200 coders have even started their first compile cycle. At that rate, coder 92 (and everyone after it) simply never gets served in time — not because the simulation is infeasible, but because **the acquisition process itself is propagating far too slowly through the ring.**

## Why this is happening

Every single dongle acquisition attempt across all 200 threads goes through one global `scheduler_lock`. Combined with your current polling design (`usleep(2000)` + re-lock + re-check on every iteration), you now have up to 200 threads all repeatedly contending for one mutex, most of them just to be told "not your turn yet." This creates massive **lock contention and OS scheduling overhead** that has nothing to do with the actual simulation logic — it's an artifact of the implementation not scaling, not a legitimate case of "the coders structurally couldn't make it in time."

This is a genuine problem, not just a slow-but-correct run: the guide's own stated expectation is that a feasible case (huge margin, like this one) *must* complete without burnout. Test 5 directly contradicts that.

## What this means for moving forward

I'd hold off calling "Easy" fully passed until this is understood, since it reveals a real scalability limitation that the subject's evaluation guide would likely also probe (the guide explicitly allows up to 200 coders as a boundary you're expected to handle). A few directions, not mutually exclusive:

1. **Reduce the polling overhead** — e.g., a slightly larger `usleep` interval reduces churn, but that's a band-aid, not a fix; it trades false-negative burnout risk for slightly less CPU thrash.
2. **This is exactly where the heap conversion would help** — right now, even under FIFO, every waiting thread still has to fully re-lock and re-check `fr_both_free`/`fr_has_priority` from scratch on every poll cycle, regardless of whether anything relevant changed. A proper heap-backed structure combined with condition-variable signaling (rather than blind polling) would let threads sleep until specifically notified their situation changed, dramatically cutting the wasted contention at high `n`.
3. **Re-test at intermediate scales** (e.g., n=20, n=50, n=100) to find where exactly this degradation starts becoming severe enough to cause false burnouts — that'll tell you if 200 is a hard requirement you need to actually fix for, or if it's more of an edge case worth noting as a known limitation.
